23 heads Contract & Tendering (22) · Business Development (1)

The room, honestly

This is the room where a document lands on Monday morning that costs Suroj the bid if it is misread. Twenty-two people whose weeks are shaped by tender documents of two, three, sometimes five hundred pages — general conditions, special conditions, technical specifications, drawings, BOQ, six annexures and two addenda that arrived after the first one was already understood. Plus one business-development person at the front of the funnel who needs to decide quickly whether an opportunity is worth pursuing at all. The whole session is about the same skill applied at four points along that funnel: read fast, find what matters, spot what is missing, and draft the correspondence that gets it clarified.

The real problem, before we get to tools

The three failure modes this room has seen:

  1. An obligation missed in the general conditions. Payment terms, liquidated damages, defect-liability period. Discovered after signing.
  2. An eligibility criterion misread. Turnover requirement across the wrong number of years; similar-work definition tighter than assumed; a JV disallowed where it was assumed permitted.
  3. Two similar tenders treated as identical. Two PWDs, two water-supply projects, superficially the same scope — but the second one has a 10% variation clause and the first one does not.

Every one of these is a reading problem, not a technical problem. The technical people were competent. They ran out of time to read carefully.

Case study — The addendum that mattered

Firm K, a mid-size EPC firm in Nashik. ₹85 Cr turnover, 220 employees, three people in the tendering team. In November 2023, they were pursuing a ₹68 Cr rural water supply project with the Maharashtra Jeevan Pradhikaran. The tender document ran 340 pages including seven annexures. Four working days to submit.

By Wednesday afternoon they had extracted the obligations and confirmed eligibility against the ₹55 Cr similar-work threshold. They qualified on a single ₹58 Cr contract from 2021, with a caveat: that project's RCC reservoir was 400 KL, and the tender specified ≥ 500 KL. They noted this as a pre-bid query but the query window had closed the previous day. The team decided to bid with a covering letter explaining the near-match.

At 6:47 PM on Thursday — the last working day before submission — MJP uploaded Addendum No. 2. The bid team saw it at 8:15 PM after returning from a site visit. Six pages of dense modifications. Two hours to cutoff.

Buried on page four: modification 4.3 relaxed the RCC reservoir criterion to "capacity 300 KL or above." Firm K's 400 KL project qualified without caveat.

Nobody read the addendum thoroughly enough to see it. The covering letter went in with the original "near-match" caveat — which under the addendum was no caveat at all, but the MJP scrutiny committee treated it as a self-declared non-compliance. Bid rejected at technical stage. The winning bid was ₹4.2 Cr higher.

What would have changed with 15 minutes and Claude. Paste the addendum. Paste the original clause 3.3. Ask: "for each modification, quote the original wording and the modified wording, and state in one line what the modification does." Modification 4.3 comes back as the third row of the table. The covering letter gets rewritten in 20 minutes. Firm K's ₹58 Cr project qualifies cleanly. The rest is on the price bid.

Takeaway for the room. Read every addendum with structured extraction — not because the bid team is careless, but because a 6:47 PM addendum on the last working day is exactly when a fatigued team misses a two-line modification. Build the addendum-read into the workflow as a standing prompt. Every bid, every addendum, no exceptions.

Tool stack for this room

Claude (long-document reader) ChatGPT (file upload) NotebookLM (for multi-annexure tenders) Microsoft Word + Copilot (drafting) Perplexity (client & consultant research)

Why Claude first for tenders: the longest context window of the mainstream tools (a full 300-page tender fits comfortably), and it is unusually good at citing back to specific clause numbers when asked. ChatGPT is close and fine for shorter tenders. NotebookLM is the tool of choice when there are ten separate PDFs — annexures, addenda, consultant brief — that need to be treated as one document.

Theoretical foundation for this session

CONTEXT WINDOWS — WHY IT MATTERS

Every AI tool has a limit on how much text it can hold in mind at once — its "context window." A 300-page tender document is about 130,000–180,000 words, which fits comfortably in Claude (200,000+ tokens) but not always in older ChatGPT models. If the tool starts giving you generic answers halfway through a long document, it may have quietly stopped remembering the beginning. The habit: ask it to cite the clause number. If the citations dry up, split the document.

UPLOAD vs PASTE — WHEN TO USE EACH

  • Upload the PDF when it is more than a few pages, or when the layout matters (tables, forms).
  • Paste text when it is a short clause or an excerpt, or when the PDF has copy-protection that garbles the upload.
  • Always keep the original open in another window. When the AI cites clause 4.3.7, look at 4.3.7 yourself.

STRUCTURED EXTRACTION BEATS SUMMARY

Do not ask "summarise this tender." Ask for a table: obligation, clause, trigger, consequence. A summary is prose that reads well and forgets things. A table forces the AI to be complete or to leave a cell blank — and blank cells are what you go looking for.

  • Section 8.3.7. "Approved brands at Annexure III" — is Annexure III attached? If not, request it.
  • Price variation. The MoRTH Star Rate formula applies to 85 percent of balance value — what is the price-index base date, and which indices apply for DI pipes specifically?

Debrief. Read one full query draft aloud. Do not accept a query that reads accusatory or as a comment on the tender's quality. The tone is professional and specific: "Clause 5.14 refers to a list of approved cement manufacturers by the Engineer-in-charge. We could not locate this list in the tender documents. Kindly confirm whether the list will be issued as an addendum, or clarify the process for approval at the LoA stage."

Common pitfalls in this room

  • Confusing the tool for a lawyer. The AI helps you find ambiguity; a lawyer resolves it. The output of any of these exercises is a shortlist for the contract manager to review — not a final position.
  • Ignoring the addenda. Nearly every real tender has two or three. Load every one of them; do the extraction again after each; note what changed.
  • Prompt drift. If the tool starts giving you obligations it invented, your prompt was probably too open. Add: "Only include obligations you can quote verbatim from the document below."
  • Trusting a summary over a table. Summaries hide things. Tables force completeness.

SESSION 02 · WINNING WORK · DAY 1 AFTERNOON (SUGGESTED)

EPC Bids & Design-Build

19 heads EPC department, whole team

The room, honestly

Nineteen people who do the same reading Session 1 does — but on tenders where the design is Suroj's problem, not the client's consultant's problem. On an item-rate PWD job, the drawing is given and the risk is executing it. On an EPC job, the client hands over a Preliminary Information Brief or a Design Basis Report and Suroj writes the specification, sizes the structure, picks the make of every major component, and carries the design risk from foundation to defect-liability. That single difference — who wrote the spec — is what the whole three hours is about.

The real problem, before we get to tools

The three failure modes this room has seen:

  1. Interface holes. Civil finished on schedule; MEP shafts were 100 mm short of what the vendor's chiller needed; three weeks lost cutting a wall that had a beam in it.
  2. A spec written more generously than costed. The bid was priced at a Fe-500 default; the spec, drafted separately in a different week by a different engineer, said Fe-500D from primary producers only. Six percent margin gone.
  3. Design-stage risks not surfaced until execution. Ground conditions worse than assumed; utility relocations not scoped; approvals not sequenced. Every one of these was findable at design stage, if someone had made the list.

All three are pattern-finding problems on documents written by different people in different weeks. The AI's core strength.

Case study — The chiller room that was 100 mm short

An EPC firm in Ahmedabad, name withheld. A ₹42 Cr commercial office design-build. On track through month 8. The office block's chiller room, in the basement, had been sized and cast by the civil team based on a preliminary MEP layout provided at month 3. The MEP vendor's detailed shop drawings arrived at month 9, based on the final chiller model selected: a Trane air-cooled screw chiller with a 3,850 mm width dimension.

The cast chiller room's internal clear width, after finishes: 3,750 mm. One hundred millimetres short.

The vendor's earlier preliminary submission had cited a 3,650 mm width for a smaller chiller in the same family. The selected chiller had been changed at month 5 during value engineering to a larger unit with better COP. Nobody re-checked the room dimensions against the revised chiller. The two facts sat in two different people's inboxes for four months.

Remediation options: (a) cut and re-cast one wall of the chiller room — ₹6.2 lakh direct cost plus three weeks of delay to the HVAC start; (b) select a different chiller from a different manufacturer that fit in 3,750 mm — ₹4 lakh premium and a re-approval cycle with the consultant. They went with (b) and lost two weeks.

What would have changed with an interface matrix and a monthly re-run. The Pattern 2 prompt from this session produces an interface matrix from the DBR. In its "Civil to MEP" row, one cell reads: "chiller room dimensions handed over to MEP at month 3, based on preliminary chiller model." A discipline of re-running the matrix every time an item within it changes — the value-engineering change at month 5 — would have caused the AI to ask: "chiller model changed; has the chiller room dimension been re-verified?" A five-minute question. Two weeks saved.

Takeaway for the room. The interface matrix is not a document you produce once at the bid stage. It is a living checklist. Run it on the DBR at bid. Run it again at design freeze. Run it again whenever a major item within it changes. Each run takes ten minutes. Each run catches a different miss.

Theoretical foundation for this session

INTERFACE MATRICES — WHY THEY EXIST

Every EPC failure post-mortem discovers that someone knew about the problem in week 4 and someone else needed to know about it in week 20. An interface matrix is the deliberate crossing of everyone-who-produces-something against everyone-who-needs-it, in a table, so that the gap surfaces before it becomes a wall being demolished. AI is exceptionally good at generating a first-cut matrix from a design brief — the discipline is asking it the right way.

DESIGN-STAGE RISK REGISTERS — WHY THEY EXIST

Not the same as a project-execution risk register. A design-stage register is a list of "we do not yet know the answer to this, and here is what could go wrong if the answer is bad." Ground conditions, utility relocation, approvals, spec-versus-price gaps, vendor lead times for long-lead items, interface boundaries. AI helps by reading a Design Basis Report and asking questions a competent engineer would ask.

THE "TWO-SPEC" PROBLEM

An EPC bid contains two specifications: the technical specification (what will be built) and the priced BOQ (what has been costed). If they disagree, the technical specification wins — you build what you said. AI can be pointed at both and asked to find every line where the technical spec is stricter than what the BOQ line implies. This is the single highest-value use of AI in this room.

Prompt patterns for this room

PATTERN 1 · SPEC-VERSUS-BOQ MISMATCH

You are a senior estimation engineer at an EPC firm. TASK Below is (1) the technical specification section of our own bid, and (2) the priced BOQ. For each BOQ line, find any place where the technical specification is stricter, more branded, or more onerous than a normal reading of the BOQ line would imply. Focus on: - Grade of material (steel, concrete, aggregate) - Brand or "approved makes" lists - Testing frequency and location - Warranty period - Client witness requirements FORMAT Table: BOQ line no. | BOQ description | Spec clause | What the spec adds that the BOQ line does not price for | Cost impact (High / Medium / Low) RULES - Only flag mismatches you can quote from both documents. - Do not invent BOQ items or spec clauses. TECHNICAL SPECIFICATION: """[…]""" PRICED BOQ: """[…]"""

PATTERN 2 · INTERFACE MATRIX FROM DESIGN BRIEF

You are a design coordinator on an EPC project. TASK Read the Design Basis Report below and produce an interface matrix covering: - Civil to MEP (electrical, HVAC, plumbing, fire) - Civil to façade - Civil to precast (if any) - MEP to façade - Any client-supplied items or scope carve-outs FORMAT Table with rows = producing discipline, columns = receiving discipline. Each cell lists (a) what is handed over, (b) when in the schedule, (c) what must be true at handover for the receiving discipline to succeed. RULES - Only include interfaces implied by the scope described. - Where the brief is silent, mark "brief silent — flag for clarification". DESIGN BASIS REPORT: """[…]"""

PATTERN 3 · DESIGN-STAGE RISK REGISTER

You are helping an EPC bid team prepare a design-stage risk register before submission. TASK Read the Design Basis Report and list every risk that could materialise between contract award and construction start. For each: - Risk in one line - Which of the following categories: geotechnical, utilities, approvals, long-lead procurement, interface, price variation - Trigger (what event turns risk into cost) - Probability (H/M/L) and cost impact (H/M/L) - Mitigation you would build into the bid RULES - Only include risks supported by evidence in the brief. Do not add generic construction risks. - If a risk depends on missing information, mark it "pending clarification" and state what you would ask. DESIGN BASIS REPORT: """[…]"""

EXERCISE 3 · DESIGN-STAGE RISK REGISTERApplied · groups of four

Build the risk register from the DBR 25 min

Setup. Groups of four. Use Pattern 3 on the warehouse DBR. Each group nominates its top three risks and reads them out. The room votes on the single risk they think is most likely to be under-priced by an inexperienced bidder.

What good looks like. Groups should surface: (1) the substation-in-week-24 dependency, (2) fire NoC risk (State Fire Service delays are common and open-ended), (3) MPCB CTE/CTO — the DBR says "assumed not applicable" but the contractor confirms; that assumption should be a bid clarification, not a risk. (4) The floor is M-35 to FM2 flatness — expensive to fix if wrong; a good register puts a mitigation "engage flooring specialist at design stage" line item.

Common pitfalls in this room

  • Treating the DBR as complete. A DBR is a starting point, not a design. If the room asks the AI "what is the design?" it will invent one. The AI's job is to help identify what is missing, not to fill it in.
  • Blaming the AI for a missed interface. The AI produces a matrix from what is written. If the DBR is silent on rainwater harvesting size, the matrix will be silent on it too. The prompt should include: "mark 'brief silent — flag for clarification' where the DBR is silent."
  • Skipping the spec-BOQ check because it feels obvious. Every EPC firm has a spec-BOQ mismatch story. Running Pattern 1 as the last step before submission is the highest-ROI use of AI in this room.

Closing 20 minutes — Q&A prompts

  1. "On your most recent EPC bid, if we had run these three prompts, what would we have caught?"
  2. "Where does the design-stage risk register go after the bid is won — who owns it, and how does it change into the execution risk register?"
  3. "What is one repeatable pattern this firm loses margin on that we should build a permanent prompt for?"
