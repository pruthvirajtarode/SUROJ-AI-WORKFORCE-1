The room, honestly

The biggest room of the workshop, and the one where the data-safety rules sit the tightest. Thirty-nine people who work across forty sites' invoices, GSTR-2A reconciliations, TDS compliance, statutory returns, bank reconciliations, vendor payments, and GST/Income Tax notices. Their work is high-volume, deadline-driven, and full of scattered documents that need to be reconciled — which is a very specific match to what AI is best at. It is also the work where a leaked figure or a leaked PAN is a real breach, not a theoretical one. Both halves of that sentence carry equal weight in this room.

The real problem, before we get to tools

  1. GSTR-2A / 2B reconciliation. Every month, matching what vendors filed on their GSTR-1 against Suroj's purchase register. Invoices missing on 2A. Amounts different. HSN codes swapped. Vendor GSTIN wrong. The exercise is repetitive and prone to fatigue errors.
  2. TDS compliance. Section 194C (contractors), 194J (professional), 194Q (purchase). Correct rate. Correct threshold. Certificate of no-deduction. Return filing. Late-filing fees for a single missed vendor can be substantial.
  3. Bank reconciliation across sites. Site petty-cash reconciling to head-office ledger, with a two-week lag and paper vouchers.
  4. Notice response drafting. GST DRC-01A, income-tax 143(1), TDS 200A, PF/ESI, professional tax. Each has a template answer for a standard reason and a serious answer for a genuine issue. Getting the first draft written well is 60% of the work.

Case study — Eight days to five

An infrastructure contractor in Pune, ₹340 Cr turnover, 28 sites. Their monthly close ran on a predictable but painful cycle. Site MIS closed on the 5th. Accounts consolidated across sites through the 8th. GST reconciliation ran the 9th and 10th. TDS returns filing on the 11th. Management MIS to the Directors on the 12th. Every month, eight working days from period-end to closed books. Every month, one person on the reconciliation team worked the weekend.

In April 2024, the finance head ran a small experiment. Three volunteers from a nine-person reconciliation team spent one hour on a Saturday learning to use Excel Copilot for the GSTR-2A vs. purchase register match. On the May close, those three ran the reconciliation with Copilot; the other six ran it the traditional way, on the same data volume — roughly 480 vendor invoices for the month.

Copilot cohort: reconciliation complete in one working day. Traditional cohort: still working at the end of day two. Same accuracy on a spot-audit of 60 randomly-selected invoices. The difference was concentrated on the "amount mismatch" category — Copilot's sorted variance report let the team address the ₹5,000+ mismatches first and treat the ₹200 timing differences as a batch at the end.

By August the whole reconciliation team was on Copilot. Close cycle: 8 days → 5 days. The freed-up time was reallocated: two people to notice-response drafting (previously outsourced to the consultant, at ₹18,000 per notice), one to a monthly vendor-master audit that had been on the wishlist for two years.

What did not change. The notice draft still went to the consultant for review before submission. The GST filing still went through the tax consultant. The controls did not weaken. Only the drafting labour moved in-house.

Takeaway for the room. The pattern to notice: one hour of training, one supervised close cycle, then general adoption. Not a company-wide rollout, not a big project. One volunteer team, one visible result, then diffusion. The finance head's role was to protect the experiment, not to mandate the change.

Tool stack for this room — and what stays out

Excel + Copilot (in-tenant, safest) Claude / ChatGPT (drafting, formula help) Adobe Acrobat AI (extracting invoice text) Perplexity (statutory rate & date lookup)

Non-negotiables for this room

  • Real GSTINs, PANs, bank account numbers, vendor names, employee salary figures, and specific payment amounts do not get pasted into public tools. Excel + Copilot inside Microsoft 365 is the safer place for real data (in-tenant, not used to train the model).
  • For public-tool exercises, replace real values with placeholders. The AI is checking your logic and formulas; it does not need the real figures.
  • If the exercise requires real data, do it in Excel with Copilot enabled, not in a browser chat.

Theoretical foundation for this session

WHY EXCEL + COPILOT IS THE PRIMARY TOOL HERE, NOT A BROWSER CHAT

Two reasons. First, data safety: Copilot inside Excel operates on your file in your tenant and does not send it to a public model. Second, competence: for arithmetic on hundreds or thousands of rows, an LLM chatting in a browser is the wrong tool — it will be slower, wronger, and lossier than a formula. What Copilot in Excel does well: write the formula for you, explain what a nested formula does, generate a pivot analysis in natural language, spot outliers, draft the narrative that goes with the table. What the browser chat does well: draft the notice response, explain the statute, rewrite a paragraph for the CA's signature.

STRUCTURED FILES — WHY PDFs ARE HARD

A GST notice is a PDF. Extracting text from a well-typed PDF is easy; from a scanned or ill-typed PDF it needs OCR. Adobe Acrobat AI does both well. For the workshop, pre-extracted text is provided; in real life, the first step of the workflow is "get the notice into clean text."

TWO-PASS RECONCILIATION

The workflow that this room should walk out with: Pass 1 — Copilot in Excel groups the raw purchase register and GSTR-2A by vendor GSTIN, sums the invoice values, and produces a variance report. Pass 2 — a browser chat drafts the vendor communication for each variance category (missing on 2A, amount mismatch, wrong GSTIN, timing difference). The variance report is spreadsheet work; the communication is drafting work. Different tools, correctly used.

Case study — The variance nobody could explain

A civil contractor executing a ₹98 Cr flyover project in Karnataka. Month 14 of 24. The monthly cost review showed a 12% overrun on the "steel — reinforcement" line, on a project where reinforcement was 22% of contract value. Nobody in the review meeting could explain it. The Cost Control head took the action to investigate; two weeks later, the investigation was still open.

The head assigned an EDP resource to build a Power BI dashboard drilling into steel consumption by activity, by month, by site zone. The dashboard took three weeks. When it opened, the 12% overrun disaggregated cleanly:

  • Zone A abutments and piers: overrun 4%. Within tolerance for site-mix.
  • Zone B superstructure: overrun 2%. Effectively on budget.
  • Zone C approach slabs and RCC drains: overrun 38%. The line item was a small share of steel tonnage but a disproportionate share of the variance.

Zone C had been executed by a subcontractor on back-to-back terms. Their reported quantities matched the BOQ. Their actual consumption, tracked via the site's own consumption sheet, had run 38% higher. Nobody had reconciled the two — because the subcontractor's billing was on BOQ, not on consumption, so the accounts team saw no variance in payments.

What Zone C had been doing: over-ordering steel, drawing extra from Suroj's stores against the subcontract, and using the excess on side work outside the subcontract. Not fraud in the strict sense — the extra work was Suroj's other project — but a booking error that concealed the variance for four months.

What would have changed with a monthly Pattern 1 (BvA narrative) prompt. The overrun would have surfaced in month 11's review, not month 14's — the Pattern 1 prompt asks for causes attributable to each >5% variance row and forces a "cause not yet identified" tag on unattributable ones. Three consecutive months of "cause not yet identified" on the same head is the trigger to investigate. The dashboard-building work (three weeks) would not have been needed; the finding was structural, not visual.

Takeaway for the room. Dashboards are for pattern-seeing. The narrative prompt is for accountability. Both are useful; but if you have to pick, the accountability discipline is what surfaces variances at month 11 instead of month 14.

The QS raised the six items with the RCC designer on Monday. Two were more consequential: the cantilever cut length was wrong by 340 mm, and the anchorage of the L-bends at the corner columns was inadequate under seismic design.

Corrective cost if caught at bar-bending yard: rebar re-cut, ₹1.2 lakh. Corrective cost if caught after slab pour: hack-cut and epoxy-splice, minimum ₹34 lakh and a two-week delay to the finishes package on that floor.

The Saturday-morning exercise saved ₹32.8 lakh of expected cost — but only if you count it that way. If the drafter had caught the errors on the traditional Monday-morning review, the same cost was saved. The AI's contribution was time: catching the errors on Saturday instead of Monday morning meant the bar-bending yard had them right the first time, not the second. On a live project, that time is the money.

What did not change. The design decisions stayed with the designer. Every AI-flagged row was reviewed by a human before rebar was cut. The AI's role was to focus the human's attention on the six rows that mattered, out of 1,800.

Takeaway for the room. AI in the QS workflow is not a designer, not a checker, not a validator. It is an attention manager. It sorts 1,800 rows into "probably fine" and "worth a look" so the engineer's time goes to the second bucket. That is the entire role. Do not oversell it. Do not undersell it either.

TEXT IS NOT DRAWING

TWO PASSES ON A TAKE-OFF

Pass 1: the AI checks the arithmetic. Sum of parts equals total. Same-item quantities across floors match the pattern. Pass 2: the AI checks the coding against the spec — is item 4.2 specified at Fe-500D, and is the take-off tagged Fe-500D or plain Fe-500? Both passes are cheap. Both catch different errors.

BBS CHECKING — WHAT THE AI CATCHES

Cut lengths that don't sum to the intended member. Wrong shape codes. Overlap lengths less than 50d. Ambiguous bar marks. Diameter/spacing mismatches with the section. It does not catch design errors — if the section says T12 at 200 c/c and the design should have been T16 at 150 c/c, only the engineer will catch that.

Setup. Individual. Below are five clauses from a mixed-use building specification. Run Pattern 1 on each. Compare the queries produced across the room; the interesting differences are which clauses were treated as ambiguous by half and clear by half.

SYNTHETIC CLAUSES · Mixed-use G+8 building specification
FIVE CLAUSES FROM A CLIENT SPECIFICATION FOR AN EXERCISE Clause 5.3.4 (Waterproofing) "All wet areas, terraces and basement raft shall be waterproofed with a system of the following makes or equivalent approved: Fosroc, Sika, BASF, Krystol. The system shall carry a ten-year warranty jointly by the manufacturer and the applicator. All wet areas shall be flood-tested for 48 hours prior to tiling." Clause 6.7 (Tile finishes) "Vitrified tiles in all common areas shall be of 800 × 800 mm size, glossy finish, water absorption less than 0.08 percent conforming to IS 15622. Approved makes: Kajaria, Somany, Nitco, Orient. Colour and pattern to be selected from the manufacturer's standard chart by the Architect. Skirting to be provided in all rooms." Clause 7.2 (Aluminium windows) "External glazing shall be double-glazed units of 6-12-6 mm configuration, low-E soft coating on the second surface, argon-filled cavity, U-value not exceeding 1.8 W/m2K. Frames shall be thermally-broken aluminium, powder-coated, colour as directed." Clause 9.4 (HVAC — chiller plant) "The chilled-water plant shall be sized for the peak block load plus 15 percent redundancy. Two chillers in an N+1 configuration. Chillers to be air-cooled, screw type, from approved make. COP not less than 3.2 at ARI standard conditions." Clause 11.6 (Electrical — LED fixtures) "LED fixtures in common areas shall be of reputed make with minimum efficacy 100 lm/W, colour temperature 4000 K, CRI greater than 80, minimum rated life 30,000 hours. Fixtures shall carry a 5-year warranty. Site sample of each type shall be approved by the Architect before bulk supply."
What the room should surface. Clause 5.3.4 — "or equivalent approved" and the 10-year joint warranty need the four approved brands' willingness confirmed at bid stage. Clause 6.7 — pattern selection at execution is a variation risk; ask for a defined selection window. Clause 7.2 — "colour as directed" is the classic hidden cost; frames come in about six standard powder-coat colours, anything else is priced. Clause 9.4 — "approved make" is undefined; ARI COP 3.2 at what ambient? Clause 11.6 — "reputed make" is undefined; suggest defining a list of three brands.

What the QS produces. A row-by-row impact note quantifying each delta where possible and flagging the two that fundamentally alter the bid: the MBR STP change (a technology change, not a spec tightening) and the BMS scope expansion (a new package). These are variation items requiring a formal proposal with revised commercials, not silent absorption. The tighter items (COP, VFD, cable thickness, pump efficiency) get absorbed with a total impact estimate — ₹22-28 lakh across the package, requiring a variation request or a written acceptance of scope.

Common pitfalls in this room

  • Trusting AI on drawing reads. If you upload a drawing PDF and ask "how many columns are on this floor" — the answer will be a guess. The AI is not a drawing reader. Text/table workflows only.
  • Asking the AI to design. "What size should this beam be?" is not the question. "Check my BBS against the section I've provided" is.
  • Skipping the section-first read on a BBS. If the section is ambiguous, the BBS check tells you nothing.
