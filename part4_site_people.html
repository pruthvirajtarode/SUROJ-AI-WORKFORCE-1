Procurement & Stores

17 heads Purchase (11) · Store (6)

The room, honestly

Two ends of the same chain. Purchase issues RFQs, compares quotations, negotiates, places POs, chases delivery. Store receives, records, issues, reconciles consumption against BOQ. When they work well together the material arrives at the right rate at the right time and the reconciliation ties. When they don't, the site engineer calls at ten at night asking where the cement is.

The real problem, before we get to tools

  1. Vendor quotations that look the same but aren't. Delivery terms different, GST inclusive or exclusive, unloading in scope or not, freight extra, split delivery, payment terms 30 vs 45 days. Comparing on gross rate alone loses money.
  2. PO covering letters that miss special conditions. The negotiated free-issue provision is in an email; the PO doesn't reference it. Six weeks later, the vendor claims never to have agreed.
  3. Consumption vs BOQ variance without explanation. Cement consumption 8% above BOQ on a slab pour. Reason unknown. Waste or theft or genuine over-pour — the store record doesn't say.
  4. Follow-up email fatigue. Purchase writes the same three follow-ups a week for a slipped delivery. The tone gets sharper each time. The vendor stops reading.

Case study — The lowest quote that wasn't

An infrastructure contractor in Aurangabad, ₹180 Cr turnover. Placing an order for 12,000 rm of DI K-9 pipe for a state water-supply project. Three quotations received. The purchase manager, a 22-year veteran, ran his standard comparative statement: basic rate, delivery days, payment terms, warranty. The lowest gross rate won. Order placed on Vendor X at ₹5,180/rm.

Six weeks in, the picture soured. Vendor X's freight, quoted "at actuals," came in at ₹410/rm — the two competing vendors had quoted freight-inclusive rates at effective ₹150 and ₹180. Vendor X's delivery slipped from the promised 30 days to 62 days, causing three weeks of idle labour at the pipeline stretch. Vendor X's unloading, promised as included, turned out to be "up to gate only" — site had to arrange internal handling at ₹85/rm across the tonnage. Vendor X's warranty was 12 months from dispatch, meaning half the warranty had expired before installation.

The actual landed cost, retrospectively normalised: Vendor X = ₹6,760/rm; runner-up Vendor Y would have been ₹6,240/rm. On 12,000 rm, that is ₹62 lakh of avoidable cost. Plus the schedule impact of three weeks, plus the operational headache of the unloading arrangement.

The purchase manager was not incompetent. He was working with the standard comparative statement his firm had used for fifteen years. On big-ticket items with straightforward terms, the format works. On items with layered exclusions — freight, unloading, warranty scope, payment financing — it hides more than it reveals.

What would have changed with Pattern 1 (quotation normalisation). A 20-minute AI-assisted normalisation on the same three quotations would have produced the retrospective picture at the front, not the back. Vendor X's true landed rate — visible before order placement — would have moved the decision to Vendor Y. Not because AI is cleverer than the 22-year veteran, but because the format the AI produces is the format that surfaces the exclusions the standard template hides.

Takeaway for the room. When a vendor's quote has more exclusions than the winning quote, they are almost certainly the more expensive vendor once normalised. If you take one habit from this session: every quotation with more than three "extra at actuals" or "excluding" clauses goes through Pattern 1 normalisation before placing the PO. No exceptions.

Theoretical foundation for this session

NORMALISING QUOTATIONS BEFORE COMPARING

A quotation comparison table produced by hand almost always compares gross prices. A comparison produced with AI assistance normalises to a common basis first: freight-inclusive, GST-inclusive, at-site, with the same payment terms. The AI does the normalisation in a step you can read and sanity-check. The comparison then means something.

A PO IS A CONTRACT — TREAT IT LIKE ONE

The most-missed field on a PO covering letter at construction firms is the "special conditions" section: what was agreed in the negotiation call, what free issues apply, what retention terms hold, what the payment cycle is against a specific milestone. AI writes this section fluently from a call summary or an email thread, if you feed both to it.

CONSUMPTION VARIANCE — TWO KINDS

A cement over-consumption is either a waste story (site pours, breakage, unauthorised use) or a BOQ story (design consumed more than the item description quantified — extra pouring due to formwork tolerance, richer mix than assumed). Same variance, two very different remedies. AI helps by prompting the store keeper to attribute; it does not itself attribute.

Prompt patterns for this room

PATTERN 1 · QUOTATION NORMALISATION

You are a procurement engineer preparing a comparative statement of quotations. CONTEXT Below are three vendor quotations for the same requirement. Each is on the vendor's own format and each has different inclusions/exclusions. TASK Normalise all three onto a common basis: - Landed rate at site (freight, unloading, insurance included) - GST added at appropriate rate - Payment terms converted to their financing-cost equivalent at 12% p.a. cost of funds - Delivery time in calendar days from PO date FORMAT Table: Vendor | Item | Basic rate | Freight | Unloading | Insurance | Sub-total | GST | Landed | Payment terms | Financing adj. | Final comparable rate | Delivery days | Warranty | Notes At the end: which vendor is cheapest on the comparable basis; which is fastest; which has the shortest warranty. And which vendor's quotation had the most exclusions we normalised in. RULES - Only add adjustments you can compute from the quotations. - Where a quotation is silent (e.g. no mention of freight), ask what to assume rather than assume. QUOTATIONS: """[…]"""

PATTERN 2 · PO COVERING LETTER WITH SPECIAL CONDITIONS

You are drafting the covering letter for a purchase order. CONTEXT Below are (1) the negotiation summary from the call/email thread and (2) the standard PO template's fields. TASK Produce the covering letter and the "special conditions" section. The letter should: - Reference the RFQ, the vendor's quotation, and the negotiation outcome - State clearly what has been agreed on top of standard PO terms: price validity, delivery schedule, quality inspection window, free issues (if any), retention, payment schedule against milestones, penalty for late delivery - Confirm what is NOT included (unloading, storage, insurance beyond gate) RULES - Only include conditions actually agreed in the negotiation notes. - Do not add customary terms not discussed. - If a term was discussed but not settled, mark it "to be confirmed in writing before dispatch". NEGOTIATION NOTES: """[…]""" PO STANDARD FIELDS: """[…]"""

PATTERN 3 · CONSUMPTION VARIANCE INVESTIGATION

You are the site accountant investigating a consumption variance. CONTEXT Below is the material consumption sheet for [ITEM] at [SITE] for [PERIOD], and the BOQ-derived expected consumption for the physical work done in the same period. TASK Produce a step-by-step investigation checklist: 1. Verify the physical work done — cross-check against the progress report entered by the engineer 2. Verify the BOQ-derived expected quantity — check the derivation for computation errors 3. Check receipts vs. issues — are all issues booked against valid vouchers 4. Check for known wastage sources — spilled cement, wet-bag losses, pump-cleaning volume, off-cut steel 5. Check for unauthorised issues — private house, other site 6. Identify the most likely explanation and quantify it At the end, produce a draft note for the Project Manager stating the finding and the recovery/prevention plan. RULES - Do not accuse anyone. Present the facts. - Do not conclude "theft" unless there is evidence beyond a variance. CONSUMPTION: """[…]""" BOQ-DERIVED EXPECTED: """[…]"""

Common pitfalls in this room

  • Comparing quotations on gross rate. If you do nothing else with AI in this room, do the normalisation. It changes decisions.
  • Cut-and-paste POs. Last month's PO for the same vendor with a new item — the special conditions inherit but the negotiation was different. Rebuild the covering letter from the notes each time.
  • Concluding on variance without the adjacent-element check. Cement variance on the wall may mean the columns were charged wrongly, not that anyone stole anything. Do not name the vendor or the operator on a first pass — the AI won't, and neither should you.

Plant, Execution & Compliance

17 heads V&M (5) · Mechanical (3) · Operation (3) · EHS (3) · Precast (1) · Quality (1) · Shuttering & Formwork (1)

A note on this room. Seven small functions that in Suroj's original plan were scattered across two much larger rooms where the material wasn't about their work. Together, they share one honest problem: site-level records that must be captured accurately, compiled truthfully, and reported upward every month. Machine logbooks, PM sheets, safety observations, quality inspection checklists, formwork usage, precast production. Different subjects; same craft. That is the whole session.

Also worth checking. Quality is listed at 1 head and EHS at 3. For a firm with forty sites and three ISO certifications, that looks like head-office-only counting. If site QA/QC and safety officers are meant to be here, this room grows meaningfully and the exercises should be tightened around site records specifically.

The real problem, before we get to tools

  1. Handwritten logbooks that get typed up on the last day of the month. Errors, illegibility, missed entries, and a monthly summary that is a guess at what actually happened.
  2. Preventive maintenance schedules that drift. The 250-hour PM was due on the crawler crane; nobody logged the hour meter; nobody scheduled the PM; a bearing failed at 380 hours.
  3. Safety observations that go into a book and never come out. No trending, no categorisation, no repeat-observation flag. The pattern is only visible after the incident.
  4. Statutory documents scattered across email inboxes. MPCB consent, factory inspector renewal, boiler certificate, pollution NoC — each on a different renewal cycle, each on a different person's calendar.
  5. Monthly compliance report to head office that is really a compilation of last month's report plus what was remembered.

Case study — From calendar PM to hour-meter PM

A Pune-based excavation contractor, fleet of 22 machines. Their preventive maintenance ran on a calendar cycle: 15th of every month, workshop team went to each machine and did the "monthly PM." Some machines had operated 240 hours in the previous month; others had operated 60. Same PM for both.

Fleet breakdown data over the previous year: 47 unplanned breakdowns across the 22 machines, averaging 2.1 breakdowns/machine/year. Total downtime cost: ₹34 lakh across the year, mostly in idle labour and lost site days.

In January 2025 the plant coordinator ran a small analysis — using Excel Copilot to correlate breakdowns with hours-since-last-PM. The pattern was stark: 71% of breakdowns occurred on machines where hours-since-last-PM exceeded 380 (against a manufacturer's recommended interval of 250 hours). The calendar cycle was disguising this; a busy machine crossed 250 hours in 25 days and then ran 15 more days before its next PM.

They implemented Pattern 3 from this session: a monthly PM schedule based on hour-meter readings and manufacturer intervals, refreshed weekly. The workshop team's total man-days deployed didn't change; the sequencing did. Busy machines got serviced when they needed it, not on the 15th.

Twelve months later: breakdowns down to 24 across the 22 machines (from 47). Downtime cost ₹18 lakh (from ₹34 lakh). Net saving ~₹16 lakh; workshop cost roughly unchanged; the one-hour-a-week spent on the hour-meter refresh was the only added cost.

What did not change. The workshop's technical work stayed the same. The mechanics didn't need new training. The vendor for spares stayed the same. The change was the sequencing decision, which came from the AI-assisted schedule.

Takeaway for the room. The pattern to notice: the tool didn't do anything the plant coordinator couldn't have done manually. The value was that the AI-assisted schedule made the discipline sustainable — it took 45 minutes a week, not 4 hours a month. When a discipline is cheap enough that nobody skips it, the discipline holds. That is the whole game.

Theoretical foundation for this session

VOICE IS FASTER THAN PEN AT SITE

The most under-used AI feature at sites is voice input. A site supervisor can dictate a five-minute walk-around into a phone and get a clean typed observation register in two minutes. Nobody has to type at the end of the shift. This is a small habit change that pays back every day.

STRUCTURED CAPTURE MATTERS MORE THAN CLEAN CAPTURE

If the logbook is a paragraph, it can be summarised. If the observation is one line with a category tag, it can be counted, trended, and prioritised. This session's core discipline: every entry, however brief, gets a category. The AI generates the category list once, and the site uses it every day.

STATUTORY RENEWALS ARE A LIST, NOT A CALENDAR

A single spreadsheet with document type, authority, valid-from, valid-until, and next-action-date, sent to a shared inbox with a monthly nudge, replaces four calendars and two email folders. AI is not doing anything clever here — it's setting up the discipline once.

PATTERN 1 · LOGBOOK-TO-SUMMARY

Common pitfalls in this room

SESSION 08 · PEOPLE & COMMUNICATION · DAY 3 AFTERNOON (SUGGESTED)

People, Admin & Communication

25 heads HR & Admin (23) · Branding (2)

The room, honestly

Twenty-three HR and Admin people and two Branding people. On the surface, very different jobs — one runs the recruitment funnel, the leave register, the offer letter, the exit interview; the other runs the company's public voice on LinkedIn, the site hoarding artwork, the brochure for the next tender presentation. They are in the same room because the underlying craft is the same: write clearly, in the right register, from real information — and produce documents, presentations and content that look like Suroj Buildcon means it. This is the room where AI's drafting strength is most obvious and its risks are most human — tone, culture, plagiarism, over-polished lifelessness.

The real problem, before we get to tools

  1. Drafting from scratch under time pressure. Offer letters, warning letters, appreciation notes, announcement emails, policy circulars. Each one has a house tone that varies across the company.
  2. Policy documents that read like nobody would read them. Dense, legalistic, rarely revisited. The employee needs a two-page answer, not a twelve-page policy.
  3. Meeting minutes that turn into confusion. Decisions unclear, owners unnamed, deadlines vague.
  4. For Branding: LinkedIn / brochure content that reads generic. Every construction firm's LinkedIn looks the same: "proud to inaugurate", "successfully completed", "esteemed client". A firm at forty sites has more interesting things to say than this.
  5. Presentations that inherit last quarter's deck. Old data, updated numbers, no fresh narrative.

Case study — The policy people actually read

A ₹200 Cr civil contractor in Mumbai, 380 employees. In late 2024 they rewrote their sexual harassment (POSH) policy — the previous version, drafted in 2018 by the corporate secretary, ran 22 pages of legalese. A compliance audit revealed that 89% of employees confirmed they had "received" the policy (they had, at induction) but only 6% could accurately state the reporting mechanism or the composition of the Internal Committee.

The HR head, a two-year veteran, was asked by the Managing Director: "why does nobody know how to report?" Her honest answer: nobody had read the 22-page document.

She ran a Pattern 2 exercise from this session. The 22-page policy went through Claude with the "policy in two voices" prompt. Output: a formal 14-page policy (tightened from 22 pages, no change in substance), and a one-page employee-facing summary that answered three questions — What is harassment (with examples). What do I do if it happens to me or I see it happen. Who is on the IC and how do I contact them.

She showed both to the compliance auditor. The formal policy passed the audit. The summary was distributed as a laminated card, one per employee, kept at their workstation. She reran the internal survey six weeks later.

Reported reading: 91% (up from an unknown baseline — nobody had asked before). Correct identification of reporting mechanism: 74% (up from 6%). Correct identification of at least two IC members: 68%.

Twelve months later, three reports had been filed via the correct mechanism — two informal grievances closed at IC level, one formal complaint investigated per procedure. All three complainants said in exit interviews (after the process concluded) that the laminated card was what they had picked up when they didn't know what to do. Not the 22-page policy on the intranet. The card.

What did not change. The IC's composition, procedure, timelines and documentation stayed identical to the formal policy. The summary card was strictly consistent — the exercise's prompt explicitly forbade the summary from contradicting or exceeding the policy. Legal reviewed both in one hour.

Takeaway for the room. Every policy this room writes is really two documents. The formal one exists for the audit. The summary exists for the employee. They are equally important. AI drafts both from one source in ten minutes. If your firm publishes only the first, the second isn't optional — it doesn't exist. Fix that.

Theoretical foundation for this session

TONE IS EDITABLE

AI writes fluent English quickly. That fluency has a default — mildly corporate, mildly warm, slightly generic. The habit that this room needs: after every draft, ask the AI to rewrite for a specific tone. "Same content, warmer." "Same content, more direct." "Same content, in the voice of a founder who knows every one of the 340 site engineers." That last rewrite is where Suroj's actual voice starts to emerge.

POLICY DOCUMENTS AS TWO DOCUMENTS

Every policy this room writes is really two documents: the policy itself (long, formal, legally clean) and the employee-facing summary (short, plain, reads in ninety seconds). AI can produce both from a single source. The mistake is producing only the first.

MINUTES ARE A DECISION LOG, NOT A TRANSCRIPT

Good minutes have three sections: decisions, actions with owners and deadlines, and open questions. Not a paragraph per agenda item. AI produces this format from a raw transcript in one prompt.

BRANDING — SPECIFIC BEATS GENERIC

The AI's default LinkedIn post is generic. To fight it, feed it something specific — a real project detail, a real number, a real quote from a site engineer, a real problem solved. Then ask it to write. The output changes texture entirely.

Themes the room should surface. (1) Career pathway / growth opportunity — 3 of 8 (2, 7, and arguably 6). (2) Work-life balance / burnout — 2 of 8 (1, 4). (3) Absence of systems / operational chaos — 2 of 8 (3, 5). (4) Role clarity at hiring — 1 of 8 (8) but a serious one. (5) External reasons (family, external pay) — 2 of 8 (5, 6). The management summary should not read as a lament — it should name the two things Suroj can actually change (a career-pathway conversation for tenure > 2 years, and a stores/coordination systems investment) and mark the rest as external. If AI produces a list of ten problems, the summary is failing. Two things, with actions.

Common pitfalls in this room

  • Publishing the first draft. Always do the tone rewrite. The default output is fluent and forgettable.
  • Confidentiality in HR is not optional. Do not paste real employee names, salaries or personal details into public tools. Word + Copilot inside the tenant is the safer place for real HR data.
  • Policy without a summary. A 12-page policy nobody reads is not a policy — it's a document.
  • Minutes with soft language. "It was broadly agreed to consider…" is not a decision. AI will produce this if you don't push back. Reject it every time.
  • For Branding: generic content in a firm that does specific work. The AI will produce generic content unless you feed it specifics. Feed it specifics.

  3. "For HR: which policy is due for the two-voice treatment first?"
