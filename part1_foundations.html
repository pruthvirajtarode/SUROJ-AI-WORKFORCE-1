How to use this document

This document is written for the trainer, not the participant. Every session block is self-contained — pull it up, teach from it, and the closing 20 minutes of Q&A picks up naturally from the exercise still on screen.

The shape of a three-hour session

TimeWhat's happeningNotes

0:30 – 1:00The room's real problem, told back to themYou describe what they actually do in a week. Do not skip this — the AI content only lands after they feel understood.

Three things to say out loud in every session

One. The tool drafts, the human decides. Every output is a first draft to be checked — the same way a junior engineer's mark-up is a first draft to be checked.

Two. Data safety is not optional. There is a fixed list of things that never get pasted into a public tool (see Data safety guardrails). The list is short; read it before you go faster.

Three. If the answer looks confident but the source is your own head, be suspicious. AI is at its best when you give it the document to work from and at its worst when you ask it to invent a fact.

Files and tools we're assuming access to

Not every participant will have every tool. The exercises are written so that anyone with either a browser-based chat tool (ChatGPT / Claude) or Microsoft 365 Copilot in Word/Excel can complete them. Where a specific tool is much better for a task (e.g. NotebookLM for long tender documents), it is called out — but no exercise is blocked on it.

The 30-minute foundations block

Run this at the start of every session. It's the same block, taught the same way, because everyone in the workshop needs the same floor of understanding — but each session applies it to different work in the two-and-a-half hours that follow.

1 · What generative AI actually is, in plain terms

A large language model is a very fast pattern-matcher trained on an enormous amount of written text. When you type into it, it predicts, one small chunk at a time, what a helpful, competent person would write next, given what you have said and the entire text it has read. It is not a database, it is not a calculator, and it does not look things up unless you tell it to. That mental model — a very well-read assistant who reads whatever you give it and drafts whatever you ask — carries almost all the way through the day.

Two consequences follow immediately:

  • Give it the document. If the answer must come from a specific tender, invoice, drawing or policy — paste the document in (or upload it), then ask. Do not ask the AI to remember what "clause 42.3" says in a tender it has never seen.
  • Do not trust unsourced numbers. If it gives you a number, ask "where did that come from in the document?" — a good tool will point at the paragraph. If it cannot, the number is a guess.

2 · What it is good at, and what it is not

GOOD AT

  • Reading long documents and pulling out specific things
  • Rewriting a rough draft in a required tone
  • Producing a first-cut summary
  • Filling a template from raw notes
  • Explaining a clause, a formula, a specification
  • Turning a long meeting transcript into minutes and actions
  • Translating between English and Hindi/Marathi at drafting quality
  • Suggesting Excel formulas and checking a formula you already have

NOT GOOD AT (WITHOUT HELP)

  • Doing arithmetic on more than a few numbers — use Excel for that
  • Reading complex drawings and construction PDFs where layout matters
  • Knowing current prices, rates, GST slabs or statutory dates unless you give them
  • Following a company-specific process it has never seen
  • Remembering what you told it last week — it does not, unless you paste it in again
  • Being right when you are vague — vague prompt, vague answer

3 · Anatomy of a good prompt

A useful prompt has five parts. You do not have to list them formally — just make sure they are in there.

PartWhat it isExample
RoleWho the AI is playing"You are a contracts engineer at an Indian EPC firm."
ContextThe document or situation, pasted in or uploaded"The tender document below is for a state PWD water-supply project…"
TaskExactly what you want done"Extract every obligation on the contractor along with the clause number."
FormatHow the output should look"As a table with columns: Clause, Obligation, Trigger, Consequence."
ConstraintWhat it must not do"Do not add any obligation that is not present in the text. If unclear, mark 'ambiguous'."
// A minimal working example — reusable across most sessions You are a [ROLE] at an Indian EPC construction firm. The document below is [WHAT IT IS]. TASK [Exactly what you want done, in one or two sentences.] FORMAT [Table? Bullet list? Two-column? Word count?] RULES - Only use information present in the document. - If something is unclear, mark it "unclear" — do not guess. - Cite the section/clause number for every claim. DOCUMENT """ [paste document here] """

4 · The single most useful habit — "cite it back"

Whenever the answer is supposed to come from a document you gave it, add one line to your prompt: "For every claim, quote the exact sentence and give its clause / section / page reference." This one line separates useful AI from confident nonsense. If the tool cannot point to a source in your document, the answer is not from your document.

Facilitator note. Do this live on stage before you move to the departments. Paste a two-page synthetic document, ask a question without the cite-back rule, then re-ask with it. The room will see the difference. This is the single most sticky demonstration of the day.

5 · What to do when the answer is wrong

  1. First move: tell it exactly what is wrong. Not "this is wrong" — "the amount in row 4 does not match the source, which says ₹4,62,300."
  2. Second move: ask it to redo, with the correction applied, and to explain what changed.
  3. Third move, if it is still wrong: start over in a new chat with a tighter prompt. Long argumentative threads make the tool worse, not better.

6 · The two-tab habit

Keep the source open in one tab. Keep the AI open in the other. Every time you accept an answer, check it against the source. This is not a productivity loss — it is what makes the productivity real. A trained Suroj professional using AI properly is roughly two to three times faster on document-heavy work than the same person alone. Without the check, they are just faster at being wrong.

Prompt quality — see it, do not just hear it

The five-part anatomy sounds obvious. The room usually agrees it makes sense, then goes back to writing prompts like Google searches. This side-by-side is the demonstration that changes that. Run it live on stage with the two prompts below, on the same synthetic tender clause. Read the outputs to the room without comment. Ask which one they would sign.

PROMPT A · WHAT MOST PEOPLE WRITE FIRST

summarize this contract clause and tell me what's important

What the AI produces (representative):

This clause discusses payment terms and mobilisation advance. It is important because it affects cash flow. The mobilisation advance is 10% of the contract value. It should be secured by a bank guarantee. Recovery happens through deductions from bills. This is a standard clause in construction contracts and should be reviewed carefully.

Fluent, forgettable, tells you nothing you did not already know. No clause reference. No specific numbers cited. No actionable output.

PROMPT B · THE SAME REQUEST, PROPERLY STRUCTURED

You are a contracts engineer at an Indian EPC firm. The clause below is from a tender document. TASK Extract: - Every number stated in the clause - Every obligation on the contractor - Every trigger event and its consequence - Any ambiguity that would need a pre-bid query FORMAT Table with columns: Item | Clause reference | What it says | Our exposure RULES - Quote the clause reference verbatim. - Do not add practice that the clause does not require. CLAUSE """ Clause 6.4 A mobilisation advance not exceeding 10 percent of the accepted contract value shall be released against an unconditional bank guarantee for an equivalent amount, valid up to the end of the recovery period. The advance shall be recovered by proportionate deductions from running account bills, commencing from the bill in which cumulative work done exceeds 15 percent, and shall be fully recovered by the time cumulative work done reaches 80 percent. """

What the AI produces (representative):

ItemClauseWhat it saysOur exposure
Advance %6.4Up to 10% of accepted valueWorking capital benefit
Security6.4Unconditional BG, matching amountBG limit consumed for full period
Recovery start6.4Bill where cum. work done > 15%Roughly month 3-4
Recovery end6.4Bill where cum. work done = 80%Roughly month 14

Same clause. Five specific findings. A pre-bid query surfaced. Actionable output the contracts team can use in a review meeting.

Facilitator note. This is the single most sticky demonstration of the day. Do it before you talk about anything else. The room does not need to be convinced that AI is powerful; they need to see that a bad prompt makes it useless. Once they have seen the two outputs side by side, the five-part anatomy sells itself.

Data safety guardrails

Read this once, remember it forever. This is the same list for everyone in the company. There is no long version.

Never paste into a public AI tool

  • Anyone's Aadhaar, PAN, bank details, salary figure or personal address.
  • A live tender bid price before submission, or any competitor's price.
  • Signed contracts with clients or vendors, in their entirety, unless a redacted version.
  • Legal correspondence — notices sent to or received from lawyers.
  • Anything marked "confidential" in a document header.

Fine to paste

  • Public tender documents downloaded from a client portal (they are already public).
  • Draft internal documents, once names and figures are placeholder-swapped.
  • Anonymised summaries, redacted samples, synthetic examples.
  • Formulas, code, error messages, technical questions.
  • Anything you would already put in an email to an external consultant.

The redaction rule of thumb

Before pasting, do a five-second search-and-replace: names to [NAME], PANs to [PAN], project titles to [PROJECT], and specific amounts to round-number placeholders. The AI does not need the real figures to check your logic — it needs the shape of the problem.

A note on Microsoft 365 Copilot

If the company is on a Microsoft 365 Copilot licence, the data-handling terms are stricter — Copilot inside Word/Excel/Outlook does not use company documents to train the model, and prompts stay inside Microsoft's tenant. That is safer than a public chat tool. For the rest of the tools, treat everything as public.
