Running order, trainer's playbook, and what to do after the workshop

Not for delivery — for planning. What to decide before the day, what to say and not say on the day, and the three small follow-ups that hold most of what the workshop taught six weeks later.

Suggested running order

Not the order the sessions are numbered in — the order they should be delivered in, so momentum builds and each room borrows from the last.

FIRST01 · Tendering & Contracts
Strongest visible result available anywhere in the programme — a 300-page tender read and mapped in ten minutes. Word travels internally before Session 2 starts.
THEN02 · EPC · 04 · Cost, Planning & Systems
Both groups already comfortable with structured data. Fast adoption; the early momentum holds.
MIDDLE03 · Accounts & 05 · Design & Quantities
The two largest bodies of high-volume, detailed work. Best run once you can quote internal examples from the earlier sessions.
LAST06 · Procurement & Stores · 07 · Plant & Compliance · 08 · People & Communication
Least prior exposure to AI tools in these groups. Running them last means you arrive with worked Suroj examples from six earlier sessions rather than generic ones.

Which sessions run on which day. If the workshop is spread across three days (8/9/10), a workable slotting is: Day 1 mornings — Sessions 1 & 2 (tendering, EPC); Day 1 afternoon — Session 4 (cost/planning/EDP). Day 2 — Session 3 (accounts, the biggest room, deserves the freshest morning) then Session 5 (design/QS/DEC). Day 3 — Sessions 6, 7, 8 (procurement, plant, HR/branding). Adjust to venue and participant travel logistics.

Trainer's playbook

Not a substitute for judgement — a checklist of decisions that will otherwise get made in the room by default. Making them in advance is the difference between a workshop that produces momentum and a workshop that produces a warm feeling for a week.

Before the day

  1. Confirm the placement questions. The plan lists five open questions — MEP execution vs design, Operation/Mechanical/QS/Precast/BD placement, Quality & EHS headcount, Suroj Modular absence, Cost Control/Planning as one team or two, Accounts as one room of 39 or two of 20. Every one of these changes a specific room. Get answers before the day, in writing.
  2. Ask each department to bring one live document. A tender, a P&L, a drawing revision list, a vendor quotation, a policy draft. Not a curated one — the one actually on their desk. This is the material for the second half of each session where synthetic data hands off to real.
  3. Check tool access at the venue. Wi-Fi, whether Claude/ChatGPT sites are unblocked, whether people have M365 Copilot licences, whether laptops are personal or company. Fix any of this that is fixable a week in advance; adjust exercises for what isn't.
  4. Print two documents per participant. The one-page data-safety card. The one-page room-specific prompt library (the "prompt patterns" for their session). Everything else stays digital.
  5. Rehearse Exercise 1 of each session yourself the day before. If the model does not produce what you expect on the exact synthetic data given, adjust the prompt or the data before people see it. Nothing kills momentum like a live demo that fails.

On the day

  1. Start with the tender demo, every time. Whether Session 1 is the first session of the workshop or not, the opening 15 minutes of every session should include a very short version of the tender-reading demo. It is the single most sticky proof that AI is genuinely useful for construction work.
  2. Walk the room during exercises. Ten minutes at each cluster of laptops. What you see is worth more than what people ask.
  3. Read one participant's output aloud per exercise. Not the best one. A middle one. Praise what worked; adjust the prompt live for what didn't; show the improved output next to the first.
  4. Do not project the answer key. The facilitator notes in this document are for you, not for the room. Let the room find the errors themselves.
  5. End each session with a "one thing" commitment. Each participant writes on a card: one workflow I will use AI for next week. Keep the cards. Six weeks later they become the follow-up survey.

When something goes wrong

The tool is slow / down. Have the same exercise ready as a paper handout with the model's output pre-generated. The exercise becomes about reading the output critically, which is a legitimate exercise on its own.

Someone asks a trick question about AI's dangers. Answer plainly. AI writes fluently and sometimes wrongly. The workshop's whole discipline — cite it back, keep the source open, do not paste confidential data, human verifies — exists precisely because of this. It is not a defect of the workshop that the room raises it. It is the workshop's point.

Someone in a senior role is dismissive. Do not argue. Ask them for one document they read last week that took longer than it should have. Run one prompt on it. Do the tender demo, live, on their document. If they still don't see it, note it and move on. The room is watching.

Someone gets frustrated in the exercise. Usually the prompt is too vague. Sit with them; walk through the five-part prompt anatomy (role, context, task, format, constraint); rewrite the prompt once; try again. Frustration is a signal to slow the person down, not the group.

What to say and what not to say

SAY

  • "The tool drafts. The human decides."
  • "Give it the document."
  • "Ask it to cite the clause."
  • "Vague prompt, vague answer."
  • "If the answer looks confident but you can't check the source, don't trust it."
  • "You'll be roughly two to three times faster on document work, if you check every output."

DO NOT SAY

  • "AI will replace people." (It won't, not in this decade for this work. Also the room will disengage.)
  • "You must use AI for everything." (No. Use it where it helps.)
  • "The AI knows more than you." (It doesn't. It reads faster.)
  • "This is the future." (Empty and doesn't help anyone start on Monday.)
  • "Trust me, it works." (Show, don't ask for trust.)

After the workshop

A one-day exposure without follow-up decays to almost nothing in six weeks. Very small structural changes hold most of what was taught. The three that matter, in decreasing order of importance:

1 · A shared prompt library, per room

One document per session, living somewhere every member of that room can access — a Google Doc, a SharePoint file, a Notion page, doesn't matter. Contains the room's three-to-four saved prompts, updated as the room finds better versions. This is the single highest-leverage follow-up. It costs nothing and it converts a workshop into a habit.

2 · A four-week check-in per session

Not a lecture. A one-hour Zoom call four weeks after each session, with a specific format: three participants each share one workflow they now do with AI, and one they tried and abandoned. The trainer hears what actually worked. The room hears from peers. Nobody has to prepare a slide.

3 · One saved prompt built into a live workflow within thirty days

Ask each room to pick one prompt from the library and make it part of a monthly ritual. Cost variance narrative on the monthly MPR. Tender obligation extraction as the first step of every new-tender review. Notice response draft as the first step of every notice received. The room chooses. The prompt is used. Muscle memory forms.

A concrete outcome to aim for

Six weeks after the workshop, when asked "what has changed at Suroj because of the AI workshop," a good answer is not "everyone is using AI." A good answer is one line per room: "Tendering is doing the obligation-map on every new tender in the first hour. Accounts has cut the GST-2A reconciliation from two days to half a day. Planning writes the monthly schedule story in a chat window from the Gantt file. HR sends every new policy with a one-page summary. Sinnar site captures the daily plant log by voice at end-of-shift." Five habits, five rooms. If half of those hold, the workshop paid for itself many times over.

Case studies — follow-up that worked, follow-up that didn't

Two real-shape stories from two Indian construction firms after their own AI workshops. Names changed. Numbers approximate. The pattern is the point.

Firm A — three habits stuck

Setting. A ₹280 Cr contractor, six months after an AI literacy workshop for 120 people. The trainer had left a "prompts library" template per department; four of the seven departments used it, three did not.

What worked. The three departments that stuck were Tendering, Accounts and Cost Control. In each case, a mid-level person — not the department head, not the youngest analyst, someone in the middle — took ownership of the shared prompt document. They updated it as they found better versions. They shared it in a monthly team huddle. Nobody told them to.

The department heads didn't drive the change. They did two things right: they did not block it, and they didn't insist on a governance framework before people started using the tools. They said "yes, try it, tell me what works" and got out of the way.

Six months in: Tendering was running the obligation-map on every new tender as the first read. Accounts had cut the monthly GST-2A reconciliation from two days to half a day and reallocated the freed time to notice-response drafting. Cost Control was drafting the variance narrative in the chat window every month, feeding it the Excel tables from Copilot.

What died naturally. Procurement never took to it — the head of procurement was 58, three years from retirement, and quietly declined to change his workflow. Nobody pushed. The Store keeper kept doing things his way. Neither is a failure of the workshop; they are what happens when a change is offered rather than mandated. The three departments that did stick got most of the value.

The MD's decision at six months. Do not run a second company-wide workshop. Instead: bring an external trainer in for a focused half-day with the Procurement team specifically, six months hence, when the head of procurement had retired and his successor could set a fresh baseline. The half-day cost less than one-tenth of the original workshop. Adoption in Procurement matched the other three departments within three months.

Firm B — nothing stuck

Setting. A ₹350 Cr infrastructure firm, four months after a very similar AI literacy workshop for 180 people. Same trainer. Same materials. Same enthusiasm on the day.

What happened. The MD, energised by the workshop, commissioned a "company-wide AI strategy document" as the follow-up. Three consultants were briefed. A sixteen-page policy was produced covering approved tools, data governance, licensing, training pathways and a governance committee. The document went through legal, IT and the audit committee. Approved in month five.

By the time the policy landed, four months had passed since the workshop. Nobody remembered the prompts. The shared prompt-library documents had gone dormant. The workshop attendees had, in the absence of any structural nudge, mostly reverted to their previous workflows.

Two people — a Cost Control analyst and one bright junior in Tendering — continued to use AI in their own work, because they had liked it and did not need permission. But there was no shared library, no team habit, no diffusion. The two people became "the AI person" in their respective teams, which is precisely the anti-pattern the trainer had warned against.

The MD's realisation at eight months. The strategy document had solved a problem nobody had. Nobody had objected to AI use before the document; the document existed to enable it, but the delay meant the enabling arrived after the enthusiasm expired. The right follow-up would have been a one-page "here are the four tools we use, here is what we do and do not paste into them" note issued within a week of the workshop — the same content, one-fifteenth the length, twelve weeks earlier.

Firm B's second workshop, run at month ten. Same trainer, same materials, but this time the follow-up was baked in from day one: shared prompt documents were pre-created and pre-shared before the workshop began, one per session, in a folder every attendee could edit. The four-week check-in call was on the calendar before the workshop started. Adoption at four months post-second-workshop matched Firm A's original. The difference was structural, not motivational.

What both firms teach

One. The follow-up is a structural question, not a motivational one. Six weeks of momentum decays regardless of how good the workshop was.

Two. A one-page data-safety note within a week beats a sixteen-page strategy document within a quarter, every time.

Three. Middle-of-the-team ownership is what makes it stick. The trainer should identify — before the workshop closes — the person in each room who is going to update the shared prompt document. That person is not always the department head or the youngest analyst.

Four. Not every department will stick. Two out of three sticking is a genuine win. Trying to force the third often costs more than it saves.

Five. If the workshop was good, the second workshop six months later should be smaller and more focused — one department at a time, on their live work — not a repeat of the first.

What NOT to do

  • Do not commission a company-wide "AI strategy document" as the follow-up. It will be written by three people, read by two, and change nothing.
  • Do not measure adoption by tool licences. Measure it by workflows that changed.
  • Do not do the same workshop again in six months. By then, the room's needs will be specific. Bring individual departments in for a focused half-day on their actual work.
  • Do not let one enthusiastic person become "the AI person." The whole point of the workshop is that every room can do it itself. A single AI person becomes a bottleneck.
